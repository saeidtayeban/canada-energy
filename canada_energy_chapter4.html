Does the charging network
reach where it's needed?

Chapter 3 modeled how smart charging protects the grid at the provincial level. Chapter 4 zooms all the way into the neighbourhood: 16,283 public charging stations, mapped against 5,433 Canadian neighbourhoods by income and dwelling type. Homeowners can charge in their driveway regardless of income. Apartment renters can't — and that's exactly where the gap shows up.

Method

Three datasets, stitched together
by location.

No single StatCan table has EV counts, income, and dwelling type at the neighbourhood level. This chapter builds that view by joining three separate sources on geography rather than waiting for one that doesn't exist.

Step 1
Geo-join stations to neighbourhoods
Every station's lat/long is matched to its Aggregate Dissemination Area via a point-in-polygon spatial join against StatCan's ADA cartographic boundary file. 16,264 of 16,283 stations matched cleanly.
Step 2
Classify each neighbourhood
Every ADA is tagged by its dominant dwelling type (house vs. apartment — a proxy for whether home charging is realistic) and its income quintile, from the 2021 Census Profile.
Step 3
Model EV demand locally
StatCan vehicle registrations only go down to the province level. Each neighbourhood's EV count is modeled as its share of provincial population × the province's total registered EVs (Ch.2 data, 2024).
Step 4
Score the gap
Charging ports per 1,000 modeled EVs, computed per neighbourhood and cross-tabbed against income and dwelling density to find where need and supply are furthest apart.
Honest caveat: EV counts here are modeled, not measured — scaled down from province totals by population share, since StatCan doesn't publish registrations below the province level. Ratios and rankings in this chapter should be read as directional, not exact counts.
Income × dwelling density

Income only predicts the gap
where home charging isn't an option.

In low-density neighbourhoods (houses, driveways), charging-desert rates barely track income — most people don't need a public charger. In apartment-dominant neighbourhoods, where public charging is often the only option, the pattern flips hard.

Key finding

Among house-dominant neighbourhoods, zero-infrastructure rates are roughly flat across income — because these households mostly don't need public chargers. Among apartment-dominant neighbourhoods, where public charging is frequently the only option, the poorest quintile is 4× more likely to have zero stations than the richest. The gap tracks need, not preference — and it's showing up exactly where renters have no alternative.

Key finding

Of the 2,297 zero-infrastructure neighbourhoods, 1,503 already have real modeled EV demand — meaning EV adoption is outrunning infrastructure, not waiting for it. The worst individual gaps cluster in Ontario, British Columbia, and Newfoundland & Labrador, overwhelmingly in the lowest income quintile.

Project complete

The picture is drawn end to end.
Grid → fleet → peak → street.

Chapter 1 showed Canada's grid decarbonizing while its roads didn't. Chapter 2 modeled the EV demand shock heading for that grid. Chapter 3 showed smart charging can absorb most of that shock — but not forever, and not evenly by province. Chapter 4 closes the loop at street level: even where the grid and the fleet are accounted for, the physical charging network itself is unevenly built, and it tracks income exactly where people have no alternative.
